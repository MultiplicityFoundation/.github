Age produces characteristics changes in the brain


Age brings several characteristic changes to the brain, including:

   ● Shrinkage: The brain's overall volume decreases, starting in the 30s or 40s and
     accelerating around age 60.
   ● Cognitive Decline: Memory, processing speed, and spatial awareness often
     deteriorate, while verbal abilities and abstract reasoning may improve.
   ● Structural Changes: Specific areas like the frontal lobes and hippocampus
     shrink more than others, affecting cognitive functions.
   ● White Matter: Myelin, the protective sheath around nerve fibers, shrinks with
     age, slowing down processing and reducing cognitive function.

These changes can impact daily tasks and cognitive abilities, but older adults can still
learn new skills and improve their knowledge.

Among the 4 characteristics, which seems responsible to the weakness of the
whole-body functions?

Among the four characteristics (shrinkage, cognitive decline, structural changes, and
white matter changes), white matter changes are most responsible for overall bodily
weakness.

White matter comprises myelinated nerve fibers that facilitate communication between
different brain regions and the rest of the nervous system. When myelin shrinks or
deteriorates, the efficiency of nerve signal transmission decreases, leading to slower
processing and reduced cognitive function. This can impact motor skills, balance,
coordination, and overall body function, contributing to a sense of physical weakness

Cellular Senescence and Skin Aging

Cellular senescence is a state of permanent cell-cycle arrest that cells enter in response
to various stressors, such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, and telomere shortening.
In the skin, senescent cells accumulate over time and contribute to aging by secreting a
range of molecules collectively known as the senescence-associated secretory
phenotype (SASP). These molecules include pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines,
and matrix metalloproteinases, which degrade the extracellular matrix and promote
inflammation1. This leads to visible signs of aging, such as wrinkles, loss of elasticity,
and thinning of the skin.

Cellular Senescence and Cancer

Interestingly, cellular senescence has a dual role in cancer. Initially, it acts as a tumor
suppressor by halting the proliferation of damaged cells and preventing malignant
transformation4. However, the SASP can also create a pro-tumorigenic
microenvironment by promoting inflammation and tissue remodeling. This can facilitate
tumor progression and metastasis1. Therefore, senescent cells can paradoxically
contribute to both cancer prevention and cancer progression.

Potential Interventions

Researchers are exploring interventions to target senescent cells, known as senolytics
and senomorphics. Senolytics selectively eliminate senescent cells, while
senomorphics modulate the harmful effects of the SASP1. These therapies hold
promise for rejuvenating aged tissues and treating age-related diseases, including
cancer
