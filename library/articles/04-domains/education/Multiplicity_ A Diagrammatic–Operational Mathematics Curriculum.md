**Multiplicity: A Diagrammatic--Operational Mathematics Curriculum**
====================================================================

**1.0 Introduction and Core Philosophy**
----------------------------------------

The Multiplicity curriculum presents a strategic vision for mathematics
education rooted in a "do-first, name-later" framework. Its core purpose
is to ground mathematical understanding in tangible, physical operations
rather than abstract axioms. We treat mathematics not as a descriptive
language applied to reality, but as the inherent, lawful dynamics of
reality itself. By starting with diagrams and the lawful "moves" that
transform them, learners discover fundamental mathematical principles as
emergent properties of a system they can manipulate, explore, and test.

The curriculum is built upon a set of core philosophical pillars that
guide its design and implementation at every level, from early childhood
to advanced research.

-   **Mathematical Onticity** This principle establishes that
    > mathematics begins with doing. Instead of learning axioms or
    > verbal definitions, students start with direct
    > operations---physical or graphical moves---on diagrams. The
    > mathematical objects and their properties are discovered through
    > these lawful dynamics.

-   **Frame Relativity (The G-N-Q Triptych)** Every concept is presented
    > through three isomorphic frames of reference: (G) the geometry of
    > wires and surfaces, (N) the number theory and combinatorics of
    > counts and multiplicities, and (Q) the information and energy flow
    > of probabilistic and quantum circuits. Our pedagogical strategy
    > involves constantly toggling between these frames, teaching
    > students to translate a single underlying idea into its different
    > but equivalent representations.

-   **Experimental Falsifiability** The curriculum maintains a steadfast
    > commitment to connecting formal structures with the physical
    > world. Each unit culminates in a measurable, real-world
    > prediction---such as acoustic frequencies, optical patterns, or
    > electronic resonance---that can be tested in a laboratory setting.
    > This makes abstract concepts tangible and their consequences
    > falsifiable.

-   **Holistic, Interdisciplinary Learning** Informed by Multiplicity
    > Theory, the curriculum fosters interconnected knowledge by
    > intentionally weaving together spatial reasoning with art, music,
    > storytelling, and civic science. This approach recognizes that
    > learning is not a linear staircase but a garden that grows in all
    > directions, supporting diverse learning styles and building
    > bridges between academic knowledge and social responsibility.

### **Pedagogical Guardrails: Ensuring Safe and Inclusive Learning**

The implementation of the Multiplicity curriculum is guided by
principles designed to support diverse learners and ensure a safe,
productive learning environment. We achieve this through a framework
that prioritizes emotional and cognitive well-being alongside academic
rigor.

At the core of this approach is the **Conscious Sovereignty Layer
(CSL)**, a framework for maintaining emotional safety during challenging
cognitive work. The CSL is a trauma-aware, neurodiversity-supportive,
and Social-Emotional Learning (SEL)-rooted methodology that helps
learners and educators manage cognitive load and navigate the emotional
landscape of deep, conceptual learning.

This philosophical and pedagogical architecture is not merely abstract;
it is made concrete through the simple yet profound grammatical system
that serves as the curriculum\'s operational core.

**2.0 The Diagrammatic--Operational Grammar**
---------------------------------------------

The curriculum's strategic power comes from its "no-axiom syntax."
Mathematical correctness is not defined by adherence to a set of verbal
axioms but by the ability to reach a target state through a sequence of
allowed transformations, or "moves." This reframes the process of proof
from abstract deduction to a hands-on journey of discovery, where
learners find invariants and simplify diagrams to their essential, or
normal, forms.

### **Generators: The Building Blocks**

Learners begin with a minimal set of four fundamental graphical elements
that can be drawn to represent mathematical objects and processes.

-   **Wires:** Oriented lines representing the flow or carriage of
    > \"stuff.\"

-   **Dots (•):** Events that occur on wires, such as forking, joining,
    > copying, or deleting.

-   **Boxes:** Processes that have input wires (at the bottom) and
    > output wires (at the top).

-   **Regions:** Labeled areas that define the context or rules for a
    > diagram (e.g., a probabilistic region, a vector space, or modular
    > arithmetic).

### **Primitive Moves: The Rules of Interaction**

A set of six legal rewrite rules governs how these generators can be
combined and manipulated. These moves are the fundamental operations
from which all mathematics in this curriculum emerges.

1.  **Serial compose:** Stacking boxes vertically to represent
    > sequential processes (do-then-do).

2.  **Parallel compose:** Placing elements side-by-side to represent
    > concurrent processes (do-in-parallel).

3.  **Bend/slide:** Deforming wires and moving elements without cutting
    > or crossing, respecting the planar topology.

4.  **Copy/merge:** Duplicating or combining information flows at
    > labeled dots, a move whose legality is determined by the
    > surrounding region, establishing a critical distinction that later
    > allows learners to physically grasp the difference between
    > classical information (which can be copied freely) and quantum
    > information (where the no-cloning principle applies).

5.  **Discard/unit:** Erasing a wire or introducing one from a ground
    > symbol, a move permitted only in specific contexts like classical
    > or probabilistic regions.

6.  **Swap:** Crossing one wire over another (braiding), an operation
    > that may or may not be trivial depending on the region\'s rules.

### **Emergent Mathematical Structures**

By applying these simple moves, learners discover fundamental
mathematical properties organically, without them ever being stated as
axioms. The grammar naturally gives rise to:

-   Identity (the straight wire)

-   Associativity (re-bracketing by sliding boxes)

-   Monoidal product (placing diagrams side-by-side)

-   Symmetry/braiding (wire crossings)

-   Duals (bending wires into cups and caps)

-   Probability normalization (total flow is conserved in a stochastic
    > region)

-   Linearity (emerges when copy/merge rules are restricted)

This grammar provides the toolkit for exploring the curriculum's central
organizing ideas: the concepts of primes and multiplicity.

**3.0 Foundational Concepts: Multiplicity and Primes**
------------------------------------------------------

The concepts of indecomposability (primeness) and multiplicity are not
isolated topics but the central narrative arc of the curriculum,
providing a unifying lens to interpret everything from simple counting
to the spectral properties of complex systems. These concepts are
introduced first in tangible forms and are revisited with increasing
sophistication, applying equally to processes, geometric shapes, and
information flows.

-   **Indecomposables (Primes)** In the diagrammatic context, a prime is
    > any box or wire that cannot be decomposed into a simpler
    > combination of elements through either serial (∘) or parallel (⊗)
    > composition using the allowed moves. These are the fundamental,
    > indivisible building blocks of more complex structures.

-   **Multiplicity** This is defined as the number of parallel copies of
    > a component that appear in a diagram's most simplified, or normal,
    > form. It represents the inherent \"how-many-ness\" of a
    > constituent part within a larger system.

-   **The Spectral View** At more advanced levels, this concept is
    > connected to a spectral perspective. Every diagram can be
    > associated with a multiset of eigenmodes (like the resonant
    > frequencies of a drum). In this view, the multiplicity of a
    > component corresponds to the degeneracy---or count of identical
    > copies---of its eigenmodes under the system's allowed symmetries.

These foundational concepts provide a common thread, developed across
different age groups in a carefully structured spiral sequence.

**4.0 Spiral Scope and Sequence (K--University)**
-------------------------------------------------

The Multiplicity curriculum employs a spiral scope and sequence. In this
pedagogical model, core concepts are not taught once but are revisited
at increasing levels of formality and complexity. This approach is not a
rigid ladder but a way of tending to a garden of knowledge, revisiting
core ideas to foster deeper roots and new growth. Each level is defined
by its core competencies, key activities, and the expected learning
artifacts students will produce.

### **A. Early Childhood (Ages 4--6): "Count, Connect, Compose"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Recognize and build numbers through
    > concatenation and bundling; understand addition as composing
    > strings and multiplication as repeating a block; identify
    > equivalence through physical moves (sliding, rotating).

-   **Key Activities:** Composing processes with yarn and wooden blocks
    > in \"String Town\"; playing \"Move Bingo\" to identify
    > topologically equivalent diagrams; discovering indecomposable
    > shapes with \"Prime Blocks.\"

-   **Assessment:** Performance tasks requiring the transformation of
    > diagram A into B using legal moves; pictorial journals
    > illustrating sequential processes.

-   **Artifacts:** Photo portfolios of diagram rewrites; number walls
    > showing bundled concatenations; a \"prime zoo\" collection of
    > indecomposable blocks.

### **B. Upper Primary (Ages 7--10): "Flows & Fractions"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Translate between geometric (G), numerical
    > (N), and flow-based (Q) frames; model fractions as the splitting
    > of flows; understand symmetry as a defined set of moves.

-   **Key Activities:** Using pipe-and-marble labs to measure flow
    > conservation; braiding paper to count minimal crossings;
    > conceptualizing area as the multiplicity of parallel wires.

-   **Assessment:** Practical quizzes using snap-circuit boards to build
    > a specified flow and predict outcomes (e.g., bulb brightness
    > proportional to flow multiplicity).

-   **Artifacts:** \"Flow passports\" that document the conservation of
    > quantities across different transformations; posters illustrating
    > braid structures.

### **C. Middle School (Ages 11--13): "Symmetry, Randomness, & Ratios"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Model probability using stochastic boxes
    > where rows sum to one; identify invariants under symmetry
    > operations; represent linear functions as boxes with gain (slope)
    > and background flow (intercept).

-   **Key Activities:** Playing \"Markov Walk Mats\" on floor tiles to
    > estimate stationary distributions; generating geometric congruence
    > through polygon move-sets; measuring frequencies of harmonic
    > strings to discover integer ratios and prime factors.

-   **Assessment:** Lab reports comparing predictions to measurements,
    > with the introduction of formal error bars.

-   **Artifacts:** Classroom murals of Markov chains with measured
    > frequencies; graphs plotting frequency versus string length.

### **D. Secondary (Ages 14--18): "Linear Worlds, Groups, and Information"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Use string diagrams for linear algebra
    > (matrices as boxes, tensor products as parallel composition);
    > solve linear systems via diagrammatic Gaussian elimination;
    > understand groups as sets of moves; distinguish classical copying
    > from the no-cloning principle in quantum regions; introduce
    > complex numbers and the Fourier transform as a change-of-basis
    > box, visualizing convolution as wire-merging.

-   **Key Activities:** Simplifying circuits using ZX/ZW-lite calculus;
    > designing error-correcting codes as tiling patterns; conducting
    > optics labs to explore convolution; building electronic RLC
    > circuits to measure transfer functions.

-   **Assessment:** Mixed-mode exams featuring diagrammatic rewrite
    > sequences, lab predictions with uncertainty analysis, and short
    > formal proofs based on invariants.

-   **Artifacts:** Simplified diagrams of quantum and classical
    > circuits; murals of error-correcting codes; lab notebooks with
    > Bode plots and fitted quality (Q) factors.

### **E. Undergraduate Core (Tier I): "Categories, Probability, and Spectra"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Formalize the diagrammatic grammar as a
    > strict symmetric monoidal category; map diagrams to algebraic
    > structures (Vect, Stoch, Rel) via functors; compute spectra of
    > operators and interpret multiplicity as degeneracy; model Bayesian
    > inversion diagrammatically.

-   **Key Activities:** Functorial semantics studios where a single
    > diagram is evaluated in multiple categories; spectral labs
    > measuring eigenvalue multiplicities in graphs; information flow
    > practica building and updating probabilistic models.

-   **Assessment:** Problem sets requiring translation between pictorial
    > diagrams and matrices; lab reports comparing predicted symmetries
    > to observed spectral multiplicities.

-   **Artifacts:** Annotated problem sets demonstrating functorial
    > translation between diagrammatic and algebraic realms; formal lab
    > reports validating spectral predictions against measured data.

### **F. Advanced Undergraduate / Graduate (Tier II): "Primes, Zeta, and Physical Cavities"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Analyze indecomposables via the
    > Krull--Schmidt theorem in diagrammatic categories; use generating
    > functions (Zeta functions of diagrams) to analyze spectral
    > properties; apply operator calculus to bound compositions; work
    > with quantum circuits in diagrammatic form.

-   **Key Activities:** Designing and 3D-printing fractal resonators to
    > measure mode multiplicities; running simulations of Casimir toy
    > models; simplifying quantum algorithms using the ZX-calculus.

-   **Assessment:** Research-style reports, conference-style posters,
    > and repositories containing simulation code and measured datasets.

-   **Artifacts:** Pre-publication manuscripts suitable for peer review;
    > conference-ready posters detailing novel experimental results; and
    > fully-documented open-data repositories that adhere to civic
    > science standards.

### **G. Research Studio / Doctoral: "Multiplicity Operators & Testable Predictions"**

-   **Core Competencies:** Define and analyze multiplicity operators on
    > process algebras; relate spectral multiplicities to symmetry
    > groups and prime factorizations; design falsifiable experiments
    > linking diagrammatic primitives to measurable physical invariants.

-   **Key Activities:** Deriving submultiplicativity and stability
    > bounds for operators; studying the analytic properties of
    > diagrammatic Zeta functions; conducting experiments in BEC or RF
    > labs to control mode multiplicity via geometry.

-   **Assessment:** Manuscript-quality papers with rigorous error
    > analyses and preregistered protocols.

-   **Artifacts:** Pre-publication manuscripts and associated open
    > datasets.

These developmental stages are unified by cross-cutting themes that
ensure the curriculum remains holistic and interconnected.

**5.0 Cross-Cutting Strands and Interdisciplinary Connections**
---------------------------------------------------------------

To ensure learning is holistic and broadly applicable, the Multiplicity
curriculum intentionally integrates four cross-cutting strands across
all age levels. These strands connect the core mathematical concepts to
computation, communication, aesthetics, and social responsibility,
grounding abstract reasoning in practical and meaningful contexts.

-   **Computation** At every level, students engage with computational
    > tools. Younger learners use block-based programming to implement
    > diagrammatic rewrites, while advanced students progress to textual
    > programming and the implementation of automated theorem provers
    > that work by simplifying diagrams to their normal forms.

-   **Language** The curriculum builds linguistic fluency by progressing
    > from informal storytelling about diagrams to the use of formal
    > mathematical language. Students learn to translate their visual
    > reasoning into precise sentences and, at higher levels, into
    > specialized domain-specific languages (DSLs) or LaTeX for
    > professional communication.

-   **Art & Music** We explore aesthetic connections by treating braids
    > as a form of dance notation, analyzing the mathematics of musical
    > harmonics and prime rhythms, and designing complex tiling
    > patterns. These activities reveal the deep structural connections
    > between mathematics and the arts.

-   **Civic Science** A strong emphasis is placed on social
    > responsibility and open inquiry. Students participate in open-data
    > labs where they publish their experimental datasets for use by
    > other classes. This strand promotes a culture of replication,
    > collaboration, and science in the public interest.

The integration of these strands informs not only the curriculum\'s
content but also the architecture we use to assess student learning.

**6.0 Assessment Architecture**
-------------------------------

Our assessment philosophy is designed to measure process, discovery, and
practical application rather than the rote memorization of facts or
formulas. Assessment rubrics emphasize **reachability** (can you legally
rewrite A→B?), **invariance recognition**, and **measurement literacy**.
This approach focuses on core operational skills developed in a
laboratory and studio context.

-   **Formative Assessment** Ongoing, low-stakes assessments provide
    > feedback to guide learning. We use methods like **rewrite
    > journaling**, where students document their step-by-step
    > transformations; **gallery walks**, where students review and
    > discuss each other\'s diagrammatic solutions; and **whiteboard
    > interviews**, where students are asked to \"show the moves\" that
    > justify their reasoning.

-   **Summative Assessment** At the conclusion of units, we evaluate
    > learning through more comprehensive tasks. These include
    > **practical builds**, such as constructing a snap circuit or
    > pipe-flow system to match a diagram; **diagram-to-algebra
    > translations** that test fluency between different frames; and
    > formal **lab reports** that require fitting models to data and
    > performing a rigorous uncertainty analysis.

-   **Competency Badges** Students can earn digital or physical badges
    > to recognize mastery of key skill sets. These badges certify
    > specific competencies that are valued across the curriculum:

    -   Diagrammatic Composer: Can reliably simplify processes to their
        > normal forms.

    -   Symmetry Cartographer: Can classify shapes and processes by
        > their allowed moves and invariants.

    -   Spectral Analyst: Can extract modes, frequencies, and
        > multiplicities from data.

    -   Information Alchemist: Can correctly translate between classical
        > and quantum regions, respecting structural constraints like
        > the no-cloning principle.

Assessing these competencies often requires hands-on work, which is
supported by a dedicated set of physical tools and laboratory
environments.

**7.0 Laboratory Tracks and Experimental Falsifiability**
---------------------------------------------------------

The curriculum's core principle of experimental falsifiability is
realized through hands-on laboratory work. These labs are not an
optional add-on but a central component of the learning process. They
provide a direct, tangible link between the abstract diagrammatic
formalism and measurable physical phenomena, allowing students to test
their mathematical predictions against reality.

### **K--12 Low-Cost Laboratories**

These lab kits are designed to be accessible, affordable, and safe for
use in K--12 classrooms, providing tangible analogues for the core
concepts of flows, processes, and transformations.

-   **String & Pipe Kits:** Yarn, PVC pipes, and marbles are used to
    > model flows, conservation laws, and splitting/merging processes.

-   **Paper Optics:** Aluminum foil slits, simple lenses, and light
    > sources allow students to physically experience the duality
    > between multiplication in one domain (e.g., frequency) and
    > convolution in another (e.g., space), a cornerstone concept in
    > signal processing and physics.

-   **Snap Circuits / Arduino:** Electronic components serve as physical
    > \"boxes\" in a circuit diagram, enabling students to measure gain,
    > introduce noise, and build tangible computational processes.

### **Advanced Laboratories (Undergraduate and Research)**

For higher-level study, the laboratories employ more sophisticated
equipment to investigate the deep connections between geometry,
symmetry, and spectral properties.

-   **3D-Printed Fractal Cavities:** Students design and print
    > resonators with precisely controlled geometric motifs, then use
    > microphones or vector network analyzers (VNAs) to measure their
    > resonant frequencies and quality factors.

-   **Casimir Analogues:** Vibrating membranes with patterned boundaries
    > or other physical systems are used to explore how changes in
    > geometric constraints lead to measurable shifts in spectral
    > density.

-   **BEC/Cold-Atom Simulations:** Advanced students use numerical
    > simulations of Bose-Einstein Condensates (BECs) or cold-atom
    > systems to visualize and study mode multiplicities in quantum
    > systems.

### **Data Standards**

To promote collaboration and civic science, all laboratory tracks are
required to publish their data in a standardized comma-separated value
(CSV) format. The schema requires fields for geometry descriptors
(including prime motif counts), material parameters, the measured
spectrum, estimated quality factors (Q), and associated uncertainties.

The successful implementation of this lab-centric curriculum depends on
equipping educators with the necessary training and materials.

**8.0 Teacher Preparation and Implementation Strategy**
-------------------------------------------------------

Successful implementation of the Multiplicity curriculum requires a
pedagogical shift from traditional, axiom-based instruction to a more
dynamic, move-based facilitation. To support educators in this
transition, we have developed a comprehensive strategy encompassing
essential materials, targeted professional development, and a clear,
phased rollout plan.

### **Essential Materials**

Classrooms are equipped with a core set of physical and digital tools to
facilitate hands-on, diagrammatic exploration.

-   **Physical Tools:** Rope sets, braid cords, magnetic whiteboards
    > with snap-on nodes, card decks representing process boxes, simple
    > optics kits, and snap circuits.

-   **Digital Tools:** Laptops with a dedicated diagram editor and
    > normalization checker, along with Python/Julia notebooks for data
    > analysis and simulation.

### **Professional Development Modules**

A series of four key training modules prepares teachers to effectively
deliver the curriculum.

1.  **From Axioms to Moves:** This module focuses on the practical
    > conversion of standard mathematics lessons into engaging, hands-on
    > rewrite challenges and discovery-based activities.

2.  **Multiple Frames:** Teachers learn to design tasks that explicitly
    > require students to perform G↔N↔Q translations, building cognitive
    > flexibility and a deeper understanding of underlying concepts.

3.  **Lab Integrity:** This training provides educators with the skills
    > to guide students in proper experimental design, including
    > uncertainty estimation, error analysis, and the use of
    > pre-registration protocols.

4.  **Equity by Embodiment:** This module focuses on creating an
    > inclusive learning environment. It covers the use of kinesthetic
    > entry points to support diverse learning styles, the development
    > of multilingual diagrams that transcend language barriers, and
    > differentiated instructional strategies for neurodivergent
    > learners, including those with ADHD.

### **Phased Implementation Timeline**

The curriculum is designed for a phased rollout to ensure a smooth and
sustainable transition for schools and districts.

-   **Phase 1 (Semester 0):** Teacher professional development,
    > procurement of kits, and a pilot program in two selected grades.

-   **Phase 2 (Semesters 1--2):** Full rollout for K--8, launch of
    > secondary school electives, and the creation of a local hub for
    > advanced laboratory work.

-   **Phase 3 (Semesters 3--4):** Implementation of the undergraduate
    > core and research studio, publication of the first cross-site
    > dataset, and a community-wide exposition of student work.

-   **Phase 4 (Ongoing):** Continuous iteration on the rewrite grammar,
    > expansion of the task library, and the introduction of
    > inter-school challenges.

This practical implementation is supported by a rigorous mathematical
foundation that guarantees the curriculum\'s coherence and soundness.

**9.0 Formal Foundations (For Advanced Review)**
------------------------------------------------

This section provides a concise overview of the formal mathematical
systems that underpin the diagrammatic-operational approach, ensuring
its rigor and soundness. It is intended for curriculum developers,
academic reviewers, and advanced educators seeking to understand the
theoretical core of the framework.

-   **Rewriting System** The foundation is a free string-diagram
    > category, denoted 𝔻, generated by a set of boxes {Bᵢ} and
    > structural nodes (e.g., copy, merge, swap) subject to planar
    > isotopy and local relations. A proof is a sequence of local
    > rewrites that transforms a diagram into a normal form. The system
    > is governed by two key principles:

    -   **Soundness:** If a diagram D can be rewritten into E (D →\* E),
        > then their interpretations under any valid monoidal functor F
        > are equal (F(D) = F(E)).

    -   **Contextual Completeness:** For specific fragments used in
        > coursework (e.g., ZX-lite for qubits), the rewrite system is
        > complete, meaning any two equivalent circuits can be shown to
        > be equal through a sequence of allowed moves.

-   **Multiplicity Operator** For a diagram D whose linear semantics has
    > a spectral multiset Spec(D) = {λₖ}, the multiplicity profile is
    > νₚ(D), a technical measure related to prime symmetries. The total
    > multiplicity M(D) is the sum of the algebraic multiplicities of
    > its eigenvalues. This operator has the crucial property of
    > submultiplicativity under composition: M(AB) ≤ M(A)M(B) for
    > bounded operators A and B.

    -   **Note on νₚ(D):** The formal definition of the multiplicity
        > profile is \[Definition to be clarified from primary source
        > author\].

-   **Spectral--Geometric Prediction** The curriculum\'s laboratory
    > track is grounded in a predictive formula that connects the
    > geometry of a physical resonator to its spectral properties. The
    > quality factors of a resonator\'s modes, Q\_{α,i}, are predicted
    > to scale with its prime-weighted complexity, Γ(α) := ∏ₚ
    > p\^(νₚ(α)), where νₚ(α) counts p-fold symmetric motifs in the
    > resonator\'s shape. The specific, testable prediction is:

    -   Q\_{α,i} ≈ K · Γ(α)ᵝ, where K and β are parameters students fit
        > to their experimental data.

-   **Error & Uncertainty** All experimental work adheres to formal
    > protocols for data reporting. This includes estimating the mean,
    > standard deviation, and standard error for repeated measurements.
    > When comparing measured outcomes to predictions, students are
    > required to report residuals, check for normality (e.g., via
    > QQ-plot), and compute 95% confidence intervals.

These formal systems provide the rigorous backbone for the curriculum,
with practical reference materials available in the appendices.

### **A. Minimal Symbol Legend**

-   **straight wire:** identity process

-   **box:** a linear, probabilistic, or stochastic map

-   **green dot (•):** copy/merge node

-   **red dot (•):** plus/minus fuse node

-   **cup/cap:** a state or effect

-   **cross:** a swap or braid

-   **⊗:** parallel composition

-   **∘:** serial composition

### **B. Diagram-to-Algebra Dictionary**

-   **wire labeled X:** ↔ a vector space or set X

-   **box f:X→Y:** ↔ a matrix or probability kernel f

-   **parallel composition:** ↔ a tensor product (or Cartesian product
    > in the category of relations)

-   **bend (cup/cap):** ↔ an evaluation or coevaluation map

-   **copy node:** ↔ a comonoid structure

-   **merge node:** ↔ a monoid structure

### **C. Software & Templates**

-   **Diagram Editor:** A graphical tool for creating and manipulating
    > string diagrams.

-   **Normalization Checker:** A computational tool to automatically
    > apply rewrite rules and find the normal form of a diagram.

-   **Lab Notebooks:** Standardized templates (e.g., Python/Julia
    > notebooks) that include uncertainty calculators and plotting
    > tools.

-   **CSV Schema:** A defined data standard for reporting experimental
    > results from the laboratory tracks.
