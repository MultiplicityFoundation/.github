Multiplicity: A Diagrammatic–Operational
Mathematics Curriculum (All-Ages)
A do-first, name-later foundation for mathematics grounded in diagrams, moves, and multiplicity.
Designed for ages 4 through research level.



0) Philosophy & Design Constraints
Mathematical Onticity. Mathematics is not a description but the lawful dynamics of reality. We
therefore start with operations (moves) rather than axioms or sentences about them.

Frame Relativity (MRP). Every topic is presented in three isomorphic frames: (G) geometry of
wires and surfaces, (N) number/combinatorics of counts and multiplicities, (Q)
information/energy flow (quantum & probabilistic circuits). Learners toggle frames by
diagrammatic translation.

Experimental Falsifiability. Each unit culminates in a measurable prediction (e.g., Q-factors,
frequency shifts, combinatorial counts), connecting formal structure to laboratory artifacts
(acoustics, optics, electronics, fluid/sand tables, RF cavities, and simulation).

No-axiom Syntax. Foundations are specified by generators & moves (rewrite rules) rather than
verbal axioms. Correctness = being reachable by allowed rewrites. Proofs = normal forms and
invariants of the rewrite system.

1) Core Grammar of Mathematics (for all levels)
We fix a minimal diagrammatic grammar used across the curriculum.

Generators (things you can draw)
   ●​   Wires: oriented lines carrying “stuff.”
   ●​   Dots (•) on wires: events (fork/join/copy/delete).
   ●​   Boxes: processes with input wires (bottom) and output wires (top).
   ●​   Regions: areas labeling contexts (e.g., probability, vector space, mod p).
Primitive Moves (legal rewrites)
   1.​ Serial compose: stack boxes (do-then-do).
   2.​ Parallel compose: place side-by-side (do-in-parallel).
   3.​ Bend/slide: planar isotopy without cutting.
   4.​ Copy/merge: permitted at labeled dots when the region allows it.
   5.​ Discard/unit: erase a wire into a ground symbol (allowed only in classical/probabilistic
       regions).
   6.​ Swap: cross wires (braid) when allowed.
What emerges without stating axioms
   ●​ Identity (straight wire), associativity (re-bracketing by sliding), monoidal product
      (side-by-side), symmetry/braiding (crossings), duals (bends/cups/caps), probability
      normalization (total flow = 1 in stochastic region), linearity (boxes represent linear maps
      when copying is restricted).

Multiplicity & Primes (recurring strand)
   ●​   Indecomposables (primes) = boxes/wires that cannot be expressed as a nontrivial serial
      ∘ or parallel ⊗ decomposition by allowed moves.
   ●​ Multiplicity of a component = the number of parallel copies in its normal form.
   ●​ Spectral view: each diagram has a multiset of eigenmodes; multiplicity counts
      degeneracies of modes under allowed symmetries.

Learners never read axioms; they practice moves and observe invariants.



2) Spiral Scope & Sequence (K–∞)
Each band lists Core Competencies, Key Activities, Assessment, and Artifacts.

A. Early Childhood (Ages 4–6): “Count, Connect, Compose”
Competencies - Recognize and build numbers by concatenation of ticks and by bundling
(5-as-■■■■■, 10-as-two-bundles-of-5). - Understand addition as “putting strings together,”
multiplication as “repeat a block.” - Identify same vs different by allowed moves (slide/rotate
without tearing).

Activities - String Town: yarn wires, wooden boxes; children compose processes physically. -
Move Bingo: draw two pictures; if one can be slid into the other, shout “same!” - Prime Blocks:
blocks that only split along certain seams; children discover indecomposable shapes.

Assessment - Performance tasks: can the child transform diagram A into B using legal moves? -
Journals: picture stories showing “do-then-do.”

Artifacts - Photo portfolios of rewrites, number walls of concatenations, “prime zoo” of
indecomposable blocks.



B. Upper Primary (Ages 7–10): “Flows & Fractions”
Competencies - Translate between (G,N,Q) frames: area of rectangles ↔ repeated
concatenation ↔ parallel/serial processes. - Fractions as sharing flows: split a wire into equal
parts; equalize by merge. - Symmetry by move sets: flip/rotate puzzles define a group of moves
without naming it.

Activities - Pipe & Marble Labs: split/merge flows, measure conservation. - Paper Braids:
crossings represent swaps; count minimal crossings. - Area as Multiplicity: tiles-as-parallel
wires; perimeter as boundary length (invariant under certain moves).
Assessment - Practical quizzes with snap-circuit boards: build a given flow; predict brightness
(proportional to flow multiplicity).

Artifacts - “Flow passports” documenting conserved quantities; braid posters.

C. Middle School (Ages 11–13): “Symmetry, Randomness, & Ratios”
Competencies - Model chance as stochastic boxes: rows sum to 1; serial/parallel composition of
chance. - Identify invariants under allowed symmetries; classify with orbit diagrams. - Linear
functions as boxes; slope = gain; intercept = background flow.

Activities - Markov Walk Mats: floor tiles are states; dice choose transitions; estimate stationary
distributions by time-on-tiles. - Polygon Moves: allowed flips/rotations generate congruence;
discover minimal presentations of move-sets. - Harmonic Strings: measure frequencies; note
integer ratios and prime factors; record multiplicities of modes.

Assessment - Lab reports with predictions vs measurements; error bars introduced.
Artifacts - Class Markov chain murals with measured stationary frequencies; frequency vs length
graphs (f ∝ 1/L).



D. Secondary (Ages 14–18): “Linear Worlds, Groups, and Information”
Competencies - Work fluently with string diagrams for linear algebra: matrices as boxes; vector
spaces as labeled regions; tensor product as parallel. - Solve systems with diagrammatic
Gaussian elimination (spider fusion / copy-merge laws). - Understand groups-as-moves and
group actions on sets and diagrams; compute orbits/stabilizers via pictures. - Reason about
information: classical copying vs no-cloning (copy node forbidden in quantum region). -
Introduce complex numbers and Fourier transform as a change-of-basis box; visualize
convolution as wire-merge.

Activities - ZX/ZW-lite Calculus: color-coded copy/merge nodes; simplify circuits to normal
forms. - Error-Correcting Codes as Tilings: parity checks are boxes; syndrome wires indicate
invariants. - Optics Lab: slits and lenses as boxes; convolution vs multiplication duality. -
Electronic RLC Labs: measure transfer function |H(ω)|; identify resonant peaks and
multiplicities.

Assessment - Mixed-mode exams: (a) diagram rewrite sequences, (b) lab predictions with
uncertainties, (c) short formal proofs by invariants.

Artifacts - Simplified quantum/classical circuits; code murals; lab notebooks with Bode plots and
fitted Q-factors.



E. Undergraduate Core (Tier I): “Categories, Probability, and Spectra”
Competencies - Formalize the grammar as a strict symmetric monoidal category via
generators/relations (still presented as moves). - Map between diagrams and algebra: functors
to (Vect, ⊗), (Stoch, ⊗), (Rel, ×). - Compute spectra: eigenvalues/eigenmodes of boxes; interpret
multiplicity as degeneracy. - Probability in diagrams: Markov categories; Bayesian inversion as a
diagrammatic adjoint.

Activities - Functorial Semantics Studio: same diagram evaluated in different target categories;
observe what changes and what doesn’t. - Spectral Labs: discrete Laplacians on graphs;
measure eigenvalue multiplicities vs symmetries. - Information Flow Practicum: build
probabilistic models; perform updates as string rewrites.

Assessments & Artifacts - Problem sets translating between pictures and matrices; lab reports
comparing predicted symmetries to multiplicities in spectra.



F. Advanced Undergraduate / Graduate (Tier II): “Primes, Zeta, and Physical
Cavities”
Competencies - Indecomposables via Krull–Schmidt in diagrammatic categories (when

Assessments & Artifacts - Research-style reports; conference-style posters; repositories with

G. Research Studio / Doctoral: “Multiplicity Operators & Testable Predictions”
Competencies - Define and analyze multiplicity operators on process algebras; prove norm and

Activities - Operator Inequalities: derive submultiplicativity and stability bounds. - Zeta of a

4) Formal Cores (rigor without axioms-in-words)
4.1 Rewriting System (Foundation)
Let 𝔻 be the free string-diagram category generated by boxes {B_i} and structural nodes {copy,
merge, swap, unit, counit} subject to planar isotopy and local relations (spider fusion, yanking).
Proofs are sequences of local rewrites terminating in a normal form NF(·) unique up to planar
isotopy.

Soundness. If D →* E by rewrites, then F(D) = F(E) for any monoidal functor F into a model
category (Vect, Stoch, Rel,…).

Completeness (contextual). For the ZX-lite fragment on qubits, the rewrite system is complete
for circuit equality within the fragment used in coursework (bounded angle set). Students verify
by normalization to a canonical form.

4.2 Multiplicity Operator
For a diagram D with spectral multiset Spec(D) = {λ_k} (eigenvalues of its linear semantics),
define the multiplicity profile

[ _p(D) := #{}, p . ]

Define the total multiplicity M(D) = ∑_k m_k where m_k is algebraic multiplicity of λ_k. In
symmetry-rich families, M(D) organizes by prime-power orbits.

Submultiplicativity under composition. For bounded operators A,B, [ M(AB) M(A),M(B). ] Sketch:
algebraic multiplicities add within invariant subspaces; composition cannot increase the product
of independent orbit counts beyond Cartesian product size. (Formal proof assigned in Tier II.)

4.3 Spectral–Geometric Prediction (for labs)
Consider a family of resonators {C_α} with boundary conditions admitting a discrete spectrum
{ω_{α,i}} with quality factors Q_{α,i}. Define prime-weighted complexity of the cavity shape by [
(α) := _{p } p^{p(α)}, ] where ν_p(α) counts p-gonal or p-fold symmetric motifs in the cavity mesh.
Prediction: [ i, Q{α,i} K, (α)^{} (>0 ), ] with deviations controlled by roughness ε and loss tangent
δ: [ Q{α,i} - c_3. ] Students fit (K,β,c_1,c_2,c_3) to data from 3D-printed cavities and test
generalization on held-out shapes.
4.4 Error & Uncertainty
Given repeated measurements Q_i, estimate (Q), s, and the standard error (=s/). For frequency
shifts Δf under a perturbation with predicted effect Δf_pred, report residuals r_i=Δf_i-Δf_pred,
check normality (QQ-plot), and compute 95% CI via t_{n-1}.

🌱 Multiplicity-Based Geometry Teaching Plan
1. Embrace Multiplicity Theory

Multiplicity Theory encourages holistic, interdisciplinary learning by recognizing the
interconnectedness of knowledge domains and learning styles. In geometry, this means
combining spatial reasoning with real-world applications, storytelling, ethics, art, and
technology.

Dear Teachers
Education, in the world of multiplicity, is not a staircase—it is a garden. It grows in all directions,
at different rhythms, with different forms of flowering. It is no longer a system designed to sort
people, but to support them. Education does not begin or end at any age—it is continuous,
living, and shared. As we move closer to reaching this goal, this lesson was prepared using
educational standards that support the educational standards of grades 8th-10th.
🔺 Differentiated Strategies for Teaching Geometry
A. Visual & Kinesthetic Learners

      ●​ Tangible Models: Use building blocks, 3D printed shapes, or recycled
         materials to physically construct geometric forms.
      ●​ Gardening Geometry: Design raised beds or planting patterns using shapes
         (e.g., hexagonal garden plots) to explore area, perimeter, and tessellation.
      ●​ Movement-Based Learning: Use body-based geometry games where
         students form shapes with string or their arms.

B. Auditory Learners

      ●​ Geometry Story Circles: Tell stories that incorporate geometric concepts (e.g.,
         the “journey of a triangle”), using metaphors and analogies aligned with
         Multiplicity Theory.
      ●​ Collaborative Dialogue: Small-group math talks encourage discussion about
         different ways to solve geometry problems, building empathy and reinforcing
         concepts through social learning.

C. Logical-Mathematical Learners

      ●​ Systems Thinking: Link geometry to larger systems (e.g., urban planning or
         environmental design). Students might redesign their schoolyard using
         geometric optimization.
      ●​ Problem Challenges: Integrate logic puzzles or building design problems that
         require calculating volume or angles.

D. Artistic & Creative Learners

      ●​ Geometry Through Art: Explore mandalas, Islamic tiling, or fractals. Have
         students design geometric art with symmetry and transformation rules.
      ●​ Storyboarding: Use comic strips or animation tools to visualize and narrate
         geometric transformations like reflection or rotation.
