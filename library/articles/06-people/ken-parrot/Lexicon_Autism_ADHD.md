My Lexicon: AFD (ADHD) and Autismal (Autistic)
    I’ve built my own Lexicon to better express the reality of neurodiverse minds
— because the standard labels never capture the depth of lived experience.
Words shape perception, and perception shapes truth.
    AFD (Attention Flow Dynamics) Commonly labeled “ADHD,” but I call it
AFD: Attention Flow Dynamics. It’s not a deficit — it’s a different rhythm
of attention. Energy flows in bursts, shifting with interest and environment.
When supported, it becomes hyper-focus, creativity, and innovation.
    Autismal (Autistic) Instead of saying “Autistic,” I use Autismal — a state
of being that reflects balance, sensory depth, and pattern recognition. It’s not
a disorder, but a different equilibrium with the world. The Autismal lens sees
details others miss and finds flow where others see chaos.
    Merged Perspective AFD and Autismal together form a powerful duality:
rapid shifting focus balanced by deep immersion in pattern. It’s a cognitive
engine that is both dynamic and grounding. This dual nature should not be
pathologized, but celebrated as an original mode of intelligence.
    This is why I created my Lexicon — to move beyond deficit language and
toward truth. We deserve words that honor our reality.

    Rote Learning Can Disrupt Neurodiverse Brilliance
    Traditional classrooms often demand that students “show their work” in
long, linear steps. For many neurodiverse learners, this requirement doesn’t
reveal their thinking — it distracts from it.
    Through observations, I see learners who solve problems by mapping nested
equations — equation within equation — directly in their minds. When required
to write every step, the flow is broken. What looks “too complicated” from the
outside is often a high-level internal methodology, compressed into patterns
instead of rote sequence.
    Research supports this. Studies show that autistic and other neurodiverse
learners often rely on pattern recognition, visual-spatial reasoning, and intuitive
leaps rather than memorized sequences. When forced into rote documentation,

    Instead of asking every learner to fit the same mold, we can: - Allow flexible
evidence of thought — mapping, diagrams, verbal explanation, or digital mod-
els. - Emphasize structure and relationships over isolated procedures. - Validate
alternative methodologies as legitimate demonstrations of intelligence.
    For neurodiverse learners, rote writing isn’t always proof of understanding
— sometimes, it’s interference. Their brilliance emerges not in the steps we
expect, but in the patterns they *see.*
    Education’s challenge is to stop equating “rote” with “real.” Our role is to
recognize and nurture the authentic genius that shows itself when we let people
think — and show their thinking — in their own way.
