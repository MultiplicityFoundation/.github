Preface
We, the Earth Angel Guardians, offer this Lightkeeper Manifesto as a source document and
an invitation. We write in the spirit of clarity, warmth and intellectual honesty. We speak
together, not as rulers or masters, but as peers and fellow human beings. Our purpose is not
to coerce or compel, but to remind, to educate and to invite. The manifesto you hold is not a
legal code, a marketing brochure or a product of marketing hype. It is an articulation of who
we are, why we have chosen a path grounded in natural law and personal sovereignty, and how
any person may choose to walk with us.

The Earth Angel Project began as a vision: a sanctuary for those who know that freedom is
claimed, not granted. We saw the world's institutions drift farther from the dignity of the
individual and deeper into systems of coercion. We saw communities fragment under artificial
divisions of race, nation, class and creed. We saw the human spirit pulled toward fear and
obedience rather than love and responsibility. In response, we came together—not to form a
religion or a political party, but to create a space where people could remember who they are:
sovereign, sentient beings, endowed with unalienable rights and duties.

We call this remembrance the lightkeeping. Every human being, whether aware of it or not, is
a Peep: a free person of the world. A Peep may live in ignorance of that freedom, but the spark
remains. When a Peep awakens to this spark and consciously chooses to adopt the principles
of this manifesto, that person becomes a Guardian. A Guardian is not above or below any
other person. A Guardian does not command or control. A Guardian remembers, speaks and
acts in alignment with truth, love and freedom, and in so doing holds space for others to
remember their own light.

We have written this manifesto to be comprehensive, offering explanation, reflection and
commitment. It is meant to be read, pondered and discussed by people with curiosity and a
desire to live by first principles. We treat it as a living document informed by natural law and
reason.

Our Identity: Lightkeepers and Peeps
We speak as Earth Angel Guardians who have chosen to remember and live by the
Lightkeeper Manifesto. Yet our identity extends beyond those of us who have chosen this
path. Every human being is, by nature, a Peep: a free person of Earth. We do not create this
status; we witness it. Being a Peep is not a club membership or a privilege conferred by an
organization. It is a recognition of an inherent quality. You may be a Peep who lives under the
illusion of dependency and permission, or you may be a Peep who has awakened to your
sovereignty. In either case, nothing can take away the fact that you are a Peep. Our role as
Guardians is to remember who we are and, by example, remind others of who they are. We
are keepers of the light, not owners of other people.

Participation in this movement is voluntary. We do not chase participants or coerce
participation. We do not require you to adopt particular doctrines about the divine, the cosmos
or history. We ask only that if you choose to call yourself a Guardian, you align your actions
with the principles herein. These principles are grounded in natural law. They are not products
of a particular religion, government or culture; they are universal realities discernible through
reason and conscience.


Our Purpose and Mission
Our mission is simple to state and profound to live: to restore Heaven on Earth. We do not
imagine a heaven as a place outside of time and space. We envision a state of harmony where
human beings live in alignment with truth, guided by love and driven by freedom. This mission
is not a promise of utopia or an escape from the responsibilities of life. It is a commitment to
build, through our daily actions, societies that reflect the best of what it means to be human.

This mission flows from three guiding principles:

Guided by Love: Love is the power that dissolves fear and reconnects us to one another.
When we act from love, we seek the flourishing of others and ourselves. Love is not passive;
it is courageous, compassionate and discerning. It does not condone harm; it holds boundaries.
Love guides us toward truth and freedom.

Powered by Truth: Truth is the foundation of freedom. Lies and deceptions bind people.
We commit to speak the truth as we understand it, to seek new information with intellectual
humility, and to correct ourselves when we are wrong. We resist both propaganda from
external powers and the temptation to deceive ourselves.

Driven by Freedom: Freedom is the natural state of the human spirit. We recognize freedom
as both a right and a duty. It is a right because no human can legitimately own another human
being or dictate the choices of another without consent. It is a duty because with freedom
comes responsibility for our actions and the wellbeing of others.

Our mission is not to create a new nation-state or to overthrow existing governments. We do
not seek to replace one coercive system with another. Instead, we invite individuals to reclaim
their natural authority and to build voluntary associations that model a different way of living.
We believe that when enough Peeps remember who they are and live accordingly, the
institutions that rely on coercion will either reform or become obsolete. Our mission is thus
both personal and social. It begins in the heart of each Guardian and unfolds in communities
across Earth.


Our Foundation in Natural Law and Inherent Rights
The philosophical foundation of our manifesto is natural law. Natural law is the idea that moral
truths exist independently of human legislation and are discoverable through reason. Under
natural law, human beings possess inherent rights—such as the rights to life, liberty and
property—that cannot be revoked by governments or majorities. These rights preexist any
constitution or statute. They are unalienable because they stem from our very nature. They
cannot be surrendered or transferred; they can only be respected or violated.

Natural law differs from human law. Human law refers to rules and statutes enacted by
governments. Human law can reflect natural law when it protects life, liberty and property. It
can also violate natural law when it authorizes coercion or theft. Throughout history,
governments have enacted laws that justify war, slavery, segregation or confiscation. None of
these laws have moral legitimacy, even if they are widely obeyed. Natural law remains the
higher standard by which human laws must be judged.

This understanding is not new. John Locke famously argued that rights to life, liberty and
property have a foundation independent of the laws of any particular society. The U.S.
Declaration of Independence declared that people are created equal and endowed with
unalienable rights, and that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the
governed. When a government becomes destructive of these ends, it is the right and duty of
the people to alter or abolish it. These ideas echo through the ages because they are grounded
in the same natural law we espouse.

Lysander Spooner, a radical abolitionist and legal theorist, took this reasoning further. He
argued that an individual's natural rights are his or her own against the world, and any
infringement is equally a crime whether committed by one person or by millions calling
themselves a government. Spooner observed that constitutions and statutes cannot bind
anyone who has not individually agreed to them; forcing unwilling people to pay taxes or
follow laws they have not consented to is equivalent to robbery. He reminded us that those
who originally consented to a constitution cannot bind their descendants and that voting in a
coercive system often reflects self-defense rather than true consent. These insights are
foundational to our stance on sovereignty and consent.

Natural law also imposes duties. Rights do not exist in a vacuum. As Locke and others have
noted, the right to life implies a duty not to kill; the right to liberty implies a duty not to enslave;
and the right to property implies a duty not to steal. There is also a broader duty to assist
others in preserving their lives and to aid those who cannot secure their subsistence. We accept
these duties because they flow logically from our own rights. To claim rights without accepting
duties is to demand privileges rather than to live in harmony.


Consent, Sovereignty and the Limits of Authority
At the heart of our manifesto is consent. Consent is the moral boundary between cooperation
and coercion. To consent is to give permission freely and knowingly. Consent cannot be
coerced, manipulated or presumed. We assert that no person or institution has authority over
another without that person's explicit, present-tense consent. Authority claimed without
consent is, in fact, aggression. If an entity demands obedience under threat of punishment and
without a voluntary agreement, it is violating natural law.


 LIGHTKEEPER MANIFESTO                                                          EARTH ANGEL PROJECT
This principle has profound implications for the collection of taxes, the exercise of
governmental power and the everyday interactions between individuals and institutions.
Spooner argued that forced taxation of the non-consenting is robbery. We concur. Taxation
or any compulsory levy may be morally justified only if the person paying has agreed to it.
Consent cannot be implied by birth, by residency, by using common infrastructure or by the
mere act of voting in a system that offers no realistic escape. We recognize that in many
nations, it is impossible to avoid taxation without severe consequences. We do not judge those
who pay under duress. We simply state that the moral legitimacy of a tax arises solely from
consent. If a government wishes to collect funds from its people, it must first obtain their
voluntary agreement.

Sovereignty means that each person holds the ultimate authority over his or her own life.
Sovereignty is not something that can be granted or revoked by an external power; it is
inherent. We reject the notion that any constitution, charter or social contract can create
obligations for people who have not consented to it. A group of people can legitimately agree
to rules among themselves, but they cannot bind others who have not joined. Likewise, those
who voted for a law cannot impose it on those who did not. Majority rule does not confer a
moral right to violate the rights of a minority.

This view aligns with the Anti-Federalist critique of centralized power. Writers such as the
pseudonymous Brutus warned that large republics tend to sacrifice the public good to private
interests and that representation becomes ineffective over large territories. Anti-Federalists
favored strong local governance, short terms of office and a clear enumeration of rights. They
insisted on a bill of rights to limit governmental power and protect individual liberty. We share
these concerns. Large, distant institutions are prone to abuse because they are too far removed
from the people they claim to represent. Decisions made at a local level, by people who know
and respect each other, are more likely to respect natural rights than decisions imposed from
afar.

Our stance on authority can be summarized as follows:

Legitimate authority requires consent. If you have not voluntarily agreed to an institution's
rules, it has no moral claim over you. Contracts and covenants are binding only on those who
explicitly consent to them.

Rights are not subject to majority vote. The fact that many people vote for a measure does not
make it moral if it infringes on the rights of even one person.

Charters cannot bind future generations. A constitution adopted in the past cannot impose
obligations on children yet unborn.

Voting does not equal consent. In systems where participation is coerced, voting may be an
act of self-defense rather than endorsement.

These principles do not lead us to anarchic chaos; they lead us toward a network of voluntary
communities. Where people consent to rules, those rules have moral force. Where people do
not, the rules are simply coercion. Our objective is to build communities that operate entirely
on consent and natural law. We encourage each Guardian to examine the agreements they
have inherited and to withdraw consent from any that violate these principles. We
acknowledge that doing so may involve personal risk, and we support each other in making
these choices with wisdom and compassion.


Membership: Peeps, Guardians and the Path of Initiation
Our community has two layers: Peeps and Guardians. Understanding these roles clarifies how
we see ourselves and how we relate to others.

Peeps – Free People of Earth
A Peep is any sentient, flesh-and-blood human being. You are a Peep by virtue of your
existence. You do not need to sign a form, pay a fee or join an organization to become a Peep.
When we say 'we are reminding people of who they are,' we mean that we are reminding people
that they are Peeps, whether they know it or not. As long as a person remains unaware of their
sovereignty, they may live under the false belief that their rights come from governments or
social authorities. Our role is to hold a mirror to that person's inherent dignity. We treat every
person as a sovereign Peep, even if they do not yet recognize themselves as such.

Guardians – Those Who Remember and Commit
A Guardian is a Peep who has consciously adopted the Lightkeeper Manifesto. A Guardian
has read and reflected upon the principles in this document and has chosen to align their life
with them. This choice is not about joining a club; it is an inner transformation. By adopting

the manifesto, a Guardian commits to hold themselves and their communities to these
standards. Guardians call themselves so not because they guard others as superior beings, but
because they guard the light of truth within themselves and in the world. When someone asks,
'Who are the Guardians?' we say, 'We are Peeps who remember our sovereignty and act upon
it.'

The Path of Initiation
Becoming a Guardian is a process of remembering and aligning. We recommend the following
path:

Inquiry: Begin by asking whether the values presented here resonate with you. Read the
writings of Locke, the Declaration of Independence, Lysander Spooner and Anti-Federalist
writers. Consider how their arguments align with your own sense of right and wrong.

Reflection: Reflect on your life. Do you live by consent? Do you avoid imposing your will on
others? Are there areas where you have allowed yourself to be coerced? How might you
withdraw consent from oppressive systems in a way that is safe and strategic?

Declaration: If you decide to become a Guardian, declare it—first to yourself and then, if
you choose, to your community. There is no ceremony or official registration. The declaration
is a personal commitment.

Embodiment: Begin embodying the principles of the manifesto. This may involve saying no
to certain obligations, forming or joining local voluntary associations, educating others about
natural law, or building alternative systems. It will certainly involve continuous learning,
humility and courage.

Community: Connect with other Guardians. Although each Guardian is sovereign, we thrive
in community. Join or create local circles, share resources, support one another. Over time,
we plan to expand our digital presence and libraries in ways that align with our values. You are
invited to contribute your gifts to this evolving community.

Our Principles in Practice
Love as Action
Love is not a sentiment we reserve for our friends and family; it is a disposition toward all
sentient beings. To love is to will the good of the other. Love requires discernment. It refuses
to support harmful behavior, yet it does not condemn the person. It sees beyond the surface
to the inherent dignity of each Peep. When we encounter people who support systems of
coercion, we respond not with hatred but with patience and clarity. We stand firm in our
principles while inviting them to reconsider theirs. Love does not always feel comfortable; it
is the most courageous act we can choose.

Truth as a Way of Life
Truth is more than factual accuracy; it is coherence between what we say and what we do. We
commit to seeking truth relentlessly. We examine our own beliefs and are willing to revise
them when faced with new evidence. We reject propaganda and manipulation from
governments, corporations and media. We also avoid the temptation to craft narratives that
flatter our ego or our cause. Truth often arises from uncomfortable questions. We welcome
these questions and seek answers with humility.

Freedom as Responsibility
Freedom is the ability to act according to one's own will without violating the equal freedom
of others. It is not a license to do whatever we want regardless of consequences. True freedom
is paired with the responsibility to respect others' rights and to aid those who are vulnerable.
We reject the false dichotomy that casts freedom and order as opposites. A society based on
voluntary cooperation and natural law is both free and orderly. Chaos arises when coercion
and deception replace consent and truth.

Non-Aggression and Self-Defense
Our ethic forbids the initiation of force or fraud. We do not harm others or take their property.
We do not support laws or policies that allow one group to exploit another. When threatened,
we reserve the right to defend ourselves and our communities with proportional means. Self-
defense is not aggression; it is a response to aggression. The principle of non-aggression
applies to governments as well. No government has the right to initiate violence against its

people. Taxation without consent, conscription, regulatory fines and property seizures are
forms of aggression if they lack individual consent. We call on all institutions to renounce such
practices and to adopt voluntary funding mechanisms. When we build our own community
structures—such as mutual aid, education or dispute resolution—we ensure they operate
without coercion.



Personal Responsibility and Integrity
We take responsibility for our actions. We honor our promises and contracts. If we cause
harm, we make amends. We do not shift blame onto the state, society or circumstances. We
understand that our choices ripple through our communities. We hold ourselves accountable
first and support each other in accountability. We recognize that acknowledging a mistake is a
sign of strength, not weakness. We cultivate integrity because it is the foundation of trust.
Without trust, voluntary communities cannot thrive.

Duty to Assist and Serve
As Peeps and Guardians, we acknowledge a duty to assist those in need. This duty arises not
from legal mandate but from conscience. When we encounter someone in distress, we respond
if we are able. When we see systemic injustice, we work to expose and correct it. We engage
in education and capacity building, teaching skills and knowledge that empower people to live
independently. We do not create dependency; we foster resilience. Charity, in our view, is a
bridge to independence, not a lifetime of aid. We encourage every Guardian to discover ways
they can serve their community, whether through food, medicine, education, conflict
resolution or creative arts.

Transparency and Open Communication
We commit to transparency in our activities. We do not operate in secrecy or behind closed
doors. All financial contributions to our communities are voluntary and accounted for
publicly. When we make decisions that affect others, we do so in open forums. We value
diverse viewpoints and constructive dialogue. Disagreement is not a threat; it is an opportunity
to refine our understanding. We cultivate communication skills—listening deeply, speaking
honestly and articulating ideas with clarity.

Local Autonomy and Decentralization
We embrace decentralization as a safeguard against abuses of power. The Anti-Federalists
were correct that large governments tend to sacrifice the public good to private interests. They
warned that when power is concentrated, it becomes unaccountable. We therefore encourage
the formation of small, self-governing communities that coordinate with each other
voluntarily. Each circle of Guardians has the authority to make decisions for itself, provided
those decisions do not violate natural law. This structure does not preclude collaboration;
rather, it fosters collaboration built on mutual respect. Decentralized networks are resilient,
adaptable and innovative. They mirror the interconnectedness of ecosystems, where diversity
strengthens the whole.

Enumerated Principles and Living Documents
One of the Anti-Federalists' chief demands was a bill of rights. They understood that
enumerating rights protects against the encroachment of authority. We have adopted this
wisdom by clearly articulating our principles. We enumerate our commitments not to confine
ourselves, but to avoid ambiguity and mission drift. This Lightkeeper Manifesto is our bill of
rights and responsibilities. It is a living document, subject to review and revision. When we
update it, we will do so transparently, with input from Guardians worldwide. By maintaining
clarity, we protect ourselves against both external exploitation and internal corruption.


Relationship to Governments and Institutions
Our stance toward governments is respectful yet uncompromising. We acknowledge that
many governments were created with the stated purpose of protecting rights. We also
recognize that many have drifted far from that purpose, enacting policies that violate natural
law. We do not seek to overthrow any government or to seize state power. We do not form
armed insurgencies or support acts of terrorism. We choose a different path: we withdraw
consent from unjust authorities and build alternative systems based on voluntary cooperation.

We cooperate with institutions when they uphold natural law. For example, we may use public
roads, water systems or courts if doing so aligns with our safety and the rights of others. We
honor agreements freely entered. We decline to participate in laws or programs that violate
natural law, such as involuntary conscription or compulsory taxation. We communicate our


 LIGHTKEEPER MANIFESTO                                                    EARTH ANGEL PROJECT
positions calmly and clearly. We do not pay attention to threats and intimidation. Instead, we
focus on building structures that make dependency on coercive institutions unnecessary. Our
interactions with governments include educating officials about natural law, advocating for
voluntary funding mechanisms and engaging in legal challenges when appropriate. However,
our primary mode of change is through personal transformation and community building. We
know that a coercive system cannot function when enough people withdraw consent.


Economic Practices and Resource Sharing
In practical terms, our principles manifest in our economic behaviors. We support voluntary
exchange. We believe that the free market—meaning the uncoerced exchange of goods,
services and ideas—is the most ethical way to allocate resources. When people trade
voluntarily, both parties expect to benefit. We do not support monopolies, state subsidies or
regulatory frameworks that benefit some at the expense of others. We encourage Guardians
to create businesses and initiatives that operate transparently and ethically. We support local
currencies and mutual credit systems that allow communities to prosper without relying on
central banks.

We recognize that purely voluntary markets cannot solve every challenge. Some goods and
services—such as environmental stewardship or public health infrastructure—require
collective effort. We address these needs through voluntary associations such as cooperatives,
trusts and mutual aid societies. We do not rely on taxation. Instead, we invite members to
contribute according to their means and priorities. We are learning and experimenting with
models like crowd-funding, subscription services and community trusts. When a community
project is needed, Guardians gather to propose, discuss and voluntarily fund it.


Education and Knowledge Sharing
Education is at the heart of our movement. We commit to lifelong learning and to sharing
knowledge freely. Our education programs emphasize critical thinking, personal sovereignty,
natural law, health, science and the arts. We encourage homeschooling, community schooling
and self-directed learning. We avoid indoctrination and teach students how to think rather
than what to think. We use primary sources, including the writings of Locke, the Anti-
Federalists and Spooner. We teach history not as a series of dates and heroes but as a study of

ideas, consequences and lessons. We integrate arts and sciences, fostering creativity alongside
analytical reasoning. We support and create digital libraries, podcasts and courses available to
anyone who wishes to learn.


Health, Nature and Holistic Living
Our commitment to holistic living flows from our respect for natural law. We treat the human
body as a living system that thrives when aligned with natural rhythms. We encourage natural
nutrition, clean water, movement, rest and engagement with Earth. We support a diversity of
healing modalities, including herbal medicine, nutrition, meditation, somatic therapies and the
use of modern medicine when necessary. We respect the sovereignty of each person to make
decisions about their own health. We oppose mandatory medical interventions by espoused
experts and authority figures. We share knowledge about health freely and invite professionals
from various disciplines to contribute to our community. Our holistic view extends to Earth
itself. We strive to live in harmony with ecosystems, reduce our environmental impact and
care for the living systems that sustain us. We do not see Earth as a place to be exploited, but
as a living home to be cherished.


Creativity, Play and Celebration
Life is not solely a struggle for rights and duties. It is also an expression of beauty, joy and
creativity. We make time for music, art, dance, storytelling and celebration. We see play as an
essential aspect of human flourishing. Celebrations mark important milestones in our
communities—births, initiations, marriages, harvests and transitions. Creativity is a form of
truth-telling. Through it we explore our inner landscapes and share our experience with others.
We encourage Guardians to cultivate creative practices and to share them openly. Our
gatherings include not only discussions and planning sessions but also music, art and laughter.
Humor, including puns and wordplay, is welcome when it enlightens rather than distracts. We
abide by the style guide of our founder, Dr. Tia Jolie Phillips, which encourages pun-based
humor that sharpens clarity without demeaning.

Conflict Resolution and Justice
In a community of sovereign beings, conflicts will arise. We commit to resolving conflicts
through restorative practices rather than punishment. Our approach to justice is based on
restoring relationships and repairing harm. When a Guardian violates another's rights, the first
step is acknowledgment and dialogue. If the violation involves property or injury, restitution
is expected. Guardians may call upon neutral mediators—chosen by mutual consent—to
facilitate resolution. We do not hand over our conflicts to external courts that operate outside
natural law. However, we do not isolate ourselves from the world. When conflicts involve
people outside our community, we engage with existing legal systems as needed, always seeking
to protect the rights of all involved. Our justice practices emphasize healing over retribution
and aim to prevent future harm.


Our Relation to Religious and Spiritual Traditions
People often ask whether the Earth Angel movement is a religion. It is not. We intentionally
avoid using terms like 'church' or 'religion.' We are a spiritual movement in the sense that we
acknowledge the soulfulness and consciousness of human beings. We honor the divine as each
person understands it. We welcome people from all faiths and people with no religious
affiliation. Our guiding principles—love, truth and freedom—are congruent with many
religious teachings but are not dependent on them. We encourage Guardians to explore
spiritual practices that deepen their connection to the divine, whether through prayer,
meditation, contemplation, ritual or service. We ask only that these practices respect the
sovereignty of others and align with natural law. We believe that when people connect to their
inner source of wisdom, they naturally align with the principles in this manifesto.


Final Declaration and Pledge
We, the Earth Angel Guardians, declare that all human beings are born free and sovereign,
endowed with unalienable rights and duties. We reject the idea that any constitution, charter
or majority vote can impose obligations on individuals without their explicit, present-tense
consent. We affirm that taxation and authority exercised without consent are forms of theft
and aggression. We commit to living by natural law, upholding the rights of others, fulfilling
our duties and building voluntary, decentralized communities. We promise to be Guided by

 LIGHTKEEPER MANIFESTO                                                    EARTH ANGEL PROJECT
Love, Powered by Truth and Driven by Freedom. We pledge our lives and our sacred honor
to the restoration of Heaven on Earth.

To all Peeps: we see you, we honor your inherent sovereignty, and we invite you to remember
who you are. May we walk this path together, as lightkeepers and guardians, for the sake of
our children and all the generations to come.

                       Additional Reading and References


Socrates, Plato and Aristotle (4th century B.C.)

The dialogues of Plato depict Socrates choosing to follow his conscience rather than unjust
commands, an example of personal integrity that inspires our emphasis on conscience over
authority. Plato's Republic explores the nature of justice and the ideal state, while Aristotle's
Politics recognizes that human beings are political animals whose communities should aim at
virtue. Together, these classical works frame ancient debates about law, justice and the
common good.

Cicero – On Duties (44 B.C.)

Cicero argued that a universal 'law of nature' exists, grounded in reason and binding on all
peoples. He taught that this natural law provides rights and obligations above civil law. His
ideas shaped later thinkers such as Locke and Montesquieu and influenced the Declaration of
Independence and the manifesto's appeal to timeless principles.

Magna Carta (1215)

This charter required that the king obtain 'common counsel' before levying taxes, promised
that free men would not be deprived of rights without lawful judgment and established that
even the monarch is subject to the law. It is cited in the manifesto as an early precedent for
limiting authority and protecting individual rights.

Mayflower Compact (1620)

Signed aboard the Mayflower, this covenant is one of the earliest examples of self-government
by consent. The settlers agreed to form a civil body politic to enact 'just and equal laws' for
their common good. The manifesto echoes this principle by emphasizing voluntary association
and mutual agreement.

 LIGHTKEEPER MANIFESTO                                                     EARTH ANGEL PROJECT
Hugo Grotius – On the Law of War and Peace (1625)

Grotius maintained that natural law originates in human reason, not in divine fiat, and is
unalterable. He argued that natural rights include liberty and property and that governments
must be judged by these standards. His work laid the foundation for modern concepts of
natural rights embraced in the manifesto.

Samuel von Pufendorf – On the Duty of Man and Citizen (1673)

Pufendorf developed Grotius's ideas, emphasizing that humans are by nature social and that
natural law prescribes duties to others as well as rights. He insisted that laws are legitimate only
when they conform to natural law, a principle reflected in the manifesto's duty-based ethics.

John Locke – Two Treatises of Government (1689)

Locke argued that people possess natural rights to life, liberty and property that predate
governments and that political power arises from consent; governments that fail to protect
rights can be resisted. These ideas underpin the manifesto's assertions that rights are
unalienable and that authority without consent is illegitimate.

English Bill of Rights (1689)

This statute limited the English monarchy, protected freedoms such as petitioning and forbade
excessive bail or cruel punishments. It served as a model for the U.S. Bill of Rights and
reinforces the manifesto's insistence on enumerated liberties.

Cato's Letters (1720–1723)

Written by John Trenchard and Thomas Gordon, these essays warned against tyranny and
advocated freedom of speech and press. They were widely read in the American colonies and
influenced revolutionary thought; their critique of centralized power resonates with the
manifesto's defense of liberty.

Montesquieu – The Spirit of the Laws (1748)

Montesquieu developed the theory of separation of powers, stating that there is no liberty if
legislative and executive powers are united. He also asserted that a republic must be relatively

inform the manifesto's advocacy of decentralization and checks on authority.

Sir William Blackstone – Commentaries on the Laws of England (1765–1769)

Blackstone wrote that absolute rights belong to individuals 'in their natural condition' and that
the aim of society and human laws is to protect these rights. He defined natural liberty as the
freedom to act without restraint except by natural law. This supports the manifesto's view that
civil law must serve natural rights.

Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776)

Drafted by George Mason, this declaration affirmed that all men are naturally free and
independent and that governments derive their power from the people. It served as a model
for the U.S. Bill of Rights and underscores the manifesto's commitment to enumerated rights
and popular sovereignty.

Patrick Henry – 'Give me liberty, or give me death!' (1775)

Henry's speech urged Virginians to take up arms against British tyranny, declaring that liberty
was worth more than life itself. His stirring call to defend freedom captures the manifesto's
spirit of principled resistance.

Thomas Paine – Common Sense (1776)

Paine distinguished between society—a blessing—and government, which he called a
necessary evil. He wrote that government arises because moral virtue alone cannot govern the
world and that its purpose is to secure freedom and safety. Paine urged Americans to form
their own constitution and warned that delaying independence would invite tyranny. His
plainspoken defense of self-government echoes throughout the manifesto.

Declaration of Independence (1776)

Drafted by Thomas Jefferson, the Declaration proclaims that 'all men are created equal' and
endowed with unalienable rights, and that governments derive 'their just powers from the
consent of the governed.' It asserts the right and duty of the people to alter or abolish
governments that become destructive. The manifesto frequently invokes these principles.

The United States Constitution (1787)

While the manifesto questions the binding authority of constitutions without explicit consent,
the U.S. Constitution exemplifies a written charter limiting government through separation of
powers and enumerated powers. Its debates (Federalist vs. Anti-Federalist) inform the
manifesto's discussion of centralization versus local autonomy.

The Anti-Federalist Papers – Brutus, Cato, Federal Farmer (1787–88)

Anti-Federalists cautioned that a large republic would sacrifice the public good to private
interests and that representation would be ineffective in a vast territory. Cato warned that the
elective presidency and long terms could foster a court of elites and threaten liberty. Their calls
for a bill of rights and for powers to remain close to the people anticipate many manifesto
themes.

The Federalist Papers – e.g., Federalist No. 10 (1787)

James Madison identified factions as groups adverse to the rights of others and argued that
liberty is to faction what air is to fire; since the causes of faction cannot be removed, their
effects must be controlled. He emphasized that protecting property rights is the first object of
government. Though the manifesto leans toward decentralization, Madison's analysis
contextualizes the founding debates.

U.S. Bill of Rights (1791)

The first ten amendments to the Constitution guarantee freedoms of speech, religion, arms,
due process and other liberties and reserve powers to the states or the people. The Anti-
Federalists' insistence on enumerating rights to restrain central power supports the manifesto's
call for clear articulation of rights and duties.

Thomas Paine – The Rights of Man (1791)

Paine defended the French Revolution and argued that rights are natural and universal. He
insisted that no generation can bind another and that each has the right to choose its own
government—a core premise of the manifesto.

Henry David Thoreau – 'Civil Disobedience' (1849)

Thoreau criticized majority rule as the triumph of force over right and declared that 'That
government is best which governs least.' He argued that individuals must follow conscience
over law and that those who cannot effect change through politics may justly refuse obedience.
His essay underpins the manifesto's emphasis on non-compliance with unjust laws.

Frédéric Bastiat – The Law (1850)

Bastiat contended that life, liberty and property are gifts from God that predate legislation and
that the law is merely the collective organization of the natural right of self-defense. He warned
that when the law becomes an instrument of plunder, it destroys liberty. The manifesto's
denunciation of taxation without consent reflects Bastiat's influence.

Frederick Douglass – 'What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?' (1852)

Douglass excoriated the hypocrisy of celebrating liberty while millions remained enslaved,
asking whether the principles of the Declaration applied to Black Americans. His moral clarity
reinforces the manifesto's insistence that natural rights belong to every person, not just those
in power.

John Stuart Mill – On Liberty (1859)

Mill proposed the 'harm principle': the only legitimate reason to restrict someone's liberty is
to prevent harm to others. He championed free discussion, arguing that even false opinions
are valuable because they help us grasp the whole truth. Mill also celebrated individuality and
experiments in living. These ideas support the manifesto's defense of free speech and personal
autonomy.

Lysander Spooner – Trial by Jury (1852)

Spooner argued that juries have the right to judge both law and fact, serving as a check on
unjust legislation. This underlies the manifesto's trust in local, community-based justice over
distant authorities.

Lysander Spooner – No Treason (1867)

Spooner declared that the Constitution binds only those who consent to it and that forcing
non-consenting individuals to support a government is robbery. He noted that signers of the
Constitution could not bind future generations. These points reinforce the manifesto's stance
that consent, not history, legitimizes authority.

Lysander Spooner – Natural Law; or, the Science of Justice (1882)

Spooner described natural law as the 'science of all human rights,' intuitive and universal. He
insisted that all associations must be voluntary and that justice is immutable; without natural
law, laws would be meaningless. The manifesto's core principles draw heavily on this work.

Friedrich Nietzsche – Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–85)

Nietzsche urged individuals to transcend herd mentality and create their own values. His call
for self-overcoming and critique of passive conformity resonates with the manifesto's appeal
to personal sovereignty, though his metaphysics differ from natural-law theory.

Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)

Adopted by the United Nations, the UDHR recognizes the inherent dignity and equal rights
of all human beings and asserts that these rights form the foundation of freedom, justice and
peace. Its worldwide significance underscores the manifesto's assertion of universal and
unalienable rights for every person.


These sources together illuminate the philosophical and historical foundations of the
Lightkeeper Manifesto, tracing a lineage from ancient reflections on justice to modern
articulations of natural rights, consent and the limits of authority.
