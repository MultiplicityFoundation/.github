    Pattern Stacking and the Activation of the Internal Library: A Cognitive
Science Perspective Kenneth Parrott 2025

                                    Abstract
         This paper explores the phenomenon of “pattern stacking” and the
     subsequent activation of an individual’s internal library of knowledge.
     Drawing on principles of cognitive science, neurodiversity, and memory
     research, it argues that the integration of lifelong informational inputs—
     such as books, documentaries, and scientific media—can unconsciously
     construct a vast internal repository. Through the conscious practice of
     free thought, this repository becomes actively accessible, enabling non-
     linear synthesis and novel insight generation. This perspective highlights
     a unique cognitive pathway that differs from conventional linear learning
     models.


2    Introduction
Human cognition is traditionally studied through models of input, storage, and
retrieval. However, neurodiverse individuals often demonstrate alternative path-
ways of knowledge acquisition and utilization. The concept of “pattern stack-
ing,” defined here as the unconscious accumulation and layering of informational
fragments across time, presents a framework for understanding how disparate
knowledge streams can converge into a coherent and dynamic internal system.


3    Pattern Stacking and Implicit Memory
Exposure to thousands of information sources—magazines, documentaries, and
scientific articles—creates an extensive base of implicit memory. Unlike explicit
recall, implicit memory enables recognition of patterns without conscious effort.
This aligns with research on incidental learning, where information becomes
encoded through repeated exposure, often outside intentional study.

4    The Internal Library Concept
The “internal library” is conceptualized as a metaphor for the brain’s intercon-
nected memory networks. Cognitive science research suggests that memories are
stored in distributed neural assemblies, linked by associative pathways. Over
time, repeated pattern stacking strengthens these pathways, creating a vast, la-
tent archive. While this archive may remain dormant in conventional contexts,
neurodiverse cognitive styles allow for its intentional activation.


5    Free Thought and Non-Linear Retrieval
A defining feature of this process is the use of free thought, rather than struc-
tured study, to access the internal library. Free thought resembles mind-wandering
but with heightened sensory and pattern awareness. Studies in creativity and
divergent thinking demonstrate that such states can facilitate remote associa-
tive retrieval, enabling connections between previously unlinked domains. For
individuals with monotropic focus, this process may operate at an amplified
level, as attention narrows onto specific threads until deeper structures reveal
themselves.


6    Fractal Knowledge Structures
Unlike linear knowledge acquisition, pattern stacking creates a fractal-like archi-
tecture. Each informational node branches into multiple associations, allowing
one pattern to unlock many. This dynamic mirrors the concept of spreading
activation in semantic networks but with greater density and depth due to the
cumulative nature of stacked patterns. The resulting structure enables rapid
cross-domain synthesis, supporting advanced problem solving and insight gen-
eration.


7    Discussion
Pattern stacking and internal library activation demonstrate how neurodiverse
cognition can yield unique epistemological advantages. Rather than viewing
information as static, this approach emphasizes its living, self-organizing quality.
The process reflects both the strengths of implicit memory systems and the
creative power of associative retrieval. Future research should investigate how
these mechanisms can be supported in educational contexts, particularly for
learners whose cognitive styles thrive on non-linear integration.

8     Evidence Base
8.1    Pattern Stacking and Implicit Learning
Research in implicit and statistical learning demonstrates that humans contin-
uously extract regularities from environmental input, often without conscious
awareness. This process contributes to language acquisition, complex skill devel-
opment, and lifelong knowledge accumulation. These findings support the idea
that sustained exposure to diverse informational sources creates a cumulative,
layered archive of patterns (Conway, 2020; Perruchet & Pacton, 2006).

8.2    Internal Library and Memory Integration
Neuroscience shows that the hippocampus and medial prefrontal cortex work
together to integrate overlapping experiences into organized memory structures.
This process transforms memory from passive storage into an interconnected
network that can be actively navigated, aligning with the concept of an internal
library (Schlichting & Preston, 2015; Zeithamova, Schlichting, & Preston, 2012).

8.3    Free Thought and Non-Linear Retrieval
Studies on mind-wandering and creative incubation reveal that free thought can
enhance problem solving by enabling remote associative retrieval. Functional
imaging has demonstrated that the Default Mode Network couples with exec-
utive control systems during idea generation, reframing mind-wandering as a
productive retrieval mechanism (Baird et al., 2012; Beaty et al., 2015).

8.4    Fractal Knowledge Structures
Semantic memory is organized as a small-world, scale-free network, where a few
central hubs connect to many peripheral nodes. This architecture mirrors the
fractal model of knowledge, where one pattern can branch into multiple asso-
ciations. Additionally, brain activity demonstrates scale-free dynamics across
temporal scales, reinforcing the fractal metaphor (Steyvers & Tenenbaum, 2005;
He, 2014).

8.5    Monotropic Focus and Neurodiverse Cognition
Monotropism theory describes a neurodiverse cognitive style characterized by
intense, narrow focus on specific interests. Recent empirical work shows that
hyper-focus is linked to enhanced associative access and creativity. This per-
spective highlights the epistemological advantages of neurodiverse cognition, re-
framing focus as a strength rather than a deficit (Murray et al., 2005; Mottron,
2017).




                                       3
8.6    Schema Effects and Lifelong Stacking
Research on schema-consistent learning shows that new information integrates
more effectively when it aligns with existing mental frameworks, while schema
disruption can also produce novel insight. This provides a scientific explanation
for the experience of recognition and reorganization within the internal library
(van Kesteren et al., 2012).

8.7    Mechanistic Pathways
The Complementary Learning Systems framework demonstrates how the hip-
pocampus rapidly encodes episodes while the cortex abstracts long-term knowl-
edge. This dual process aligns with the way pattern stacking evolves into gener-
alizable insights. Neural coupling between hippocampal and prefrontal regions
further explains integration across time (McClelland, McNaughton, & O’Reilly,
1995; Preston & Eichenbaum, 2013).


9     Conclusion
The discovery of one’s internal library represents not only a personal cogni-
tive awakening but also a model of how knowledge may be internally organized
and dynamically accessed. Pattern stacking, free thought, and fractal retrieval
illustrate a powerful neurodiverse approach to cognition—one that shifts learn-
ing from passive accumulation to active, integrative activation. Scientific evi-
dence across memory research, creativity studies, and neurodiversity frameworks
strongly supports this model, affirming its relevance for both cognitive science
and educational practice.
