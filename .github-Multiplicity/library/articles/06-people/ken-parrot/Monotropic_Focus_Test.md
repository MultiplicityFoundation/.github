   A Monotropic Focus Test (MFT):
A Rapid, Mechanism-Based Diagnostic Tool for Neurodiversity Kenneth Parrott
2025

                                   Abstract
         Current diagnostic practices for neurodivergence—particularly autism—rely
     on lengthy batteries of behavioral assessments and self-reported traits. We
     propose a concise diagnostic protocol, the Monotropic Focus Test (MFT),
     designed to capture core cognitive styles of monotropism: narrow, sus-
     tained focus; difficulty disengaging; and sensory gating failures during
     deep cognitive immersion. The MFT includes three tasks: (1) Positive →
     Negative → Positive Recovery, (2) Special Interest Deep Dive → Abrupt
     Disruption, and (3) Auditory Response During Deep Dive. Empirical
     evidence suggests autistic individuals show slower disengagement from
     visual targets (e.g., in the gap–overlap paradigm), differential reward pro-
     cessing for special-interest stimuli, and altered auditory-evoked potentials
     (e.g., MMN/P300). These converge to validate the MFT’s potential as
     a fast, objective diagnostic tool with neurotypical baselines. If validated,
     the MFT could significantly streamline neurodivergence identification and
     shift the framing of diagnosis toward core cognitive strengths.


2    Introduction
Autism and related neurodivergent profiles are commonly characterized by monotropism,
an attentional style marked by deep, intense focus on a narrow set of interests
and reduced flexibility in shifting attention [8]. Conventional diagnostic tools of-
ten rely on indirect measures—self-reports, social-behavioral checklists, or broad
executive function tests—leading to diagnostic delays and subjective ambiguity.
    We propose a more direct, mechanism-oriented approach: the MFT, de-
signed to measure attentional transition dynamics across visual, affective, and
auditory domains. Each task reflects a hypothesized core feature of monotropic
cognition:

    1. Delayed recovery from negative stimuli (stickiness of focus)
    2. High resistance to interruption from special-interest engagement
    3. Auditory inattention during deep focus


3     Background and Supporting Evidence
Visual Disengagement
Multiple studies using gap–overlap paradigms show that autistic individuals ex-
hibit significantly longer saccadic latencies, especially under overlap conditions
requiring disengagement from a central stimulus (visual “stickiness”) [2, 10, 11].
Reduced disengagement emerges in infancy as an early ASD marker [10], while
recent findings show more pronounced deficits under repetitive-motion contexts
[11]. Auditory versions of the gap–overlap task also reveal disengagement chal-
lenges tied to LC–NE system arousal markers [5].

Special-Interest Engagement and Reward Processing
Eye-tracking and fMRI studies demonstrate strong attentional capture and
heightened reward responses to circumscribed-interest stimuli in autistic indi-
viduals, often stronger than to social or monetary cues [1, 6].

Auditory Sensory Gating and ERP Markers
Autistic individuals frequently show atypical auditory processing, including al-
tered mismatch negativity (MMN) and P300 ERP components, suggesting de-
layed or reduced orientation to salient stimuli—including one’s own name—under
conditions of deep immersion [4, 3, 9, 7].


4     Methods
Participants
    • Neurodivergent group: Individuals diagnosed with ASD and/or ADHD,
      ages 8–40.
    • Neurotypical controls: Matched on age, IQ, and education.
    • Sample size: N ≈ 30 per group for pilot validation.

Tasks and Measures
1. Positive → Negative → Positive Recovery
    • Stimuli: Individual-preferred (positive) images/sounds; brief aversive
      (negative) stimulus; return to positive.


                                        2
    • Measures: Latency to reorient, pupil dilation recovery, HR variability,
      saccade latencies.

2. Special Interest Deep Dive → Abrupt Disruption
    • Stimulus: Participant’s special-interest activity (e.g., puzzle, drawing).
    • Disruption: Unrelated task or question.

    • Measures: Latency to switch focus, error rate post-interruption, gaze
      retention on original vs. new target.

3. Auditory Response During Deep Dive
    • Stimulus: Participant’s name or distinctive tone.
    • Measures: Response rate, behavioral latency, EEG/ERP markers (MMN,
      P300).


5     Expected Results
    • ASD (monotropic): Long latencies in all tasks, high auditory non-
      response, strong engagement resistance.

    • ADHD: Shorter initial transitions with less sustained immersion; incon-
      sistent auditory responses.
    • Neurotypical: Rapid disengagement and re-engagement, consistent au-
      ditory orientation.


6     Discussion and Significance
The MFT offers a mechanism-centered, time-efficient screen for neurodivergence
that reframes diagnosis around attention style, not deficits. If validated, it could:
    1. Provide objective, quantifiable markers

    2. Reduce reliance on lengthy batteries
    3. Support early detection and strengths-oriented profiling
    4. Inform educational and occupational accommodations
