       As an individual who spent a decade working primarily independently, I have been

increasingly impressed by the power of educators when they work together; it is the finest

example of synergy I have seen.

       When it comes to student work, it is commonplace for only one teacher to review and

grade the work of only their students and only their discipline. If we apply a strong collaborative

team to review student work, there is going to be more constructive feedback for students, which

gives them more room to meet their goals and fulfill their potential. As a person who has worked

independently for so long, collaborative review sounded a bit intimidating at first. Zepeda (2019)

described how this process can be awkward at first, but it smooths out after time. Reading about

the process may decrease the intimidation factors and give people more confidence in the

process. In addition to the students’ benefit, collaborative review guides teachers to “make better

decisions relating to the curriculum and its development, instructional strategies, and

assessments” (Zepeda, 2019). There are many structured protocols that can be followed in

integrating collaborative reviewing into a school.

The “Critical Friends Protocol” (CFP) is a multifaceted protocol designed to facilitate

meaningful feedback and reflection among educators. It can be used for various purposes,

including collaboratively examining student work. Researchers have found that isolation is

indeed detrimental for teachers, and using the CFP leads to less teacher isolation and stronger

support between teachers (Zepeda, 2019). Strong teams and structure set the foundation for an

effective CFP; teams that review student work will “observe each other’s classroom, cueing into

instructional practices, student engagement, and [finally], student work as it unfolds live in the

classroom” (Zepeda, 2019).
Grade-level or subject-area teams are other forms of strong groups that serve many purposes,

with one of those purposes being effective collaborative review of student work. Through

interdisciplinary collaboration, teachers can use current and historical events to create projects

that will stimulate students’ critical thinking. When all teachers in a particular subject are

analyzing student work, the combined masterminds of the subject will produce valuable insight

from multiple perspectives; in turn, this collaborative group has more power to contribute to

positive educational change, as opposed to teachers sticking to analyzing student work from their

own classes.

       I propose we add this element to grade-level teams to review student work

collaboratively. This will strengthen teams and is a great way for teachers to support one another.

By focusing on a shared group of students, teachers can collaborate more effectively and share

insights and strategies tailored to their specific context. I have been collaborating in a grade-level

community of practice for several months and feel as though it would significantly contribute to

teamwork and reduce teacher isolation, and in turn, have a positive impact on student learning.

Holding short weekly grade-level meetings to collaborate on ways to improve instruction and

support with the mutual aspect of having the same groups of students. Monthly meetings with all

grade-level groups could be held to share their findings and perceptions.

                                      Reciprocal Peer Coaching

       One type of peer coaching is reciprocal peer coaching, which is particularly beneficial for

experienced teachers seeking collaborative professional development opportunities. In this

model, pairs of teachers take turns observing each other's classrooms, providing constructive

feedback, and engaging in reflective discussions to enhance instructional practices. Reciprocal

peer coaching relies on mutual trust and respect, making it well-suited for educators with a solid

foundation in teaching who value peer input and dialogue.

                                              Peer mentoring

       Another type of peer coaching is peer mentoring, which is ideally suited for beginning

teachers navigating the complexities of the profession. In this model, an experienced teacher

serves as a mentor to provide guidance, support, and practical advice to a less experienced

colleague. Peer mentoring involves regular meetings, classroom observations, and targeted

coaching sessions focused on specific areas of growth identified by the mentee. This approach

fosters a supportive relationship that empowers new teachers to develop their teaching skills and

build confidence in their practice.

                                           Collaborative Inquiry

       A third type of peer coaching is collaborative inquiry, which benefits both mentors and

mentees as they engage in joint exploration of teaching practices and student learning. This
model is well-suited for educators at any career stage who are interested in collaborative inquiry

and continuous improvement. Collaborative inquiry involves collaborative planning, data

analysis, action research, and reflection on instructional practices and their impact on student

outcomes. By working together to investigate teaching and learning processes, participants

deepen their understanding of effective pedagogy and contribute to a culture of ongoing

professional growth within their school community. Overall, peer coaching in its various forms

promotes collegiality, shared learning, and improved teaching practices, ultimately leading to

enhanced student achievement.
